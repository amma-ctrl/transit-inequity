Left Waiting: Inequity in America's Transit Powerhouses

Visualizing access gaps and service disparities across income in the Bay Area & NYC

Amen Hailu INFO 609 Final Project · Pratt Institute · 2025 ArcGIS Pro · ArcGIS Dashboards GTFS · American Community Survey

Key findings

A bus stop nearby doesn't mean the bus comes often.

Measured by service frequency rather than stop proximity alone, access to federally funded transit is far from equitable in either region.

Infrequent service clusters in lower-income communities

Low-frequency stops concentrate along the East Bay corridor in Richmond and San Leandro, and in the Bronx, southeast Queens, and Staten Island in NYC.

The Bay Area has far larger access voids than NYC

Large unserved zones appear in Contra Costa County, outer Alameda, southern Marin, and the South Bay. NYC's few voids sit on the city's outskirts.

Isolation from rail compounds long waits

NYC's low-frequency clusters are often geographically isolated from the subway, requiring long waits or multiple transfers to reach other boroughs.

Income and investment don't line up neatly

Bayview shows service gaps despite recent gentrification, suggesting a complex interplay between income and transit investment.

Findings by region

Two transit powerhouses, two kinds of gaps

San Francisco Bay Area

Low-frequency service

Low-frequency stops cluster along the East Bay corridor, especially in Richmond and San Leandro. These communities not only have large low-to-moderate income populations but are also historically underserved by high-frequency transit options. Some areas, like Bayview, show service gaps despite recent gentrification, suggesting a complex interplay between income and transit investment.

Low-frequency service

The Bronx, southeast Queens, and much of Staten Island exhibit large clusters of low-frequency stops, particularly from bus routes with infrequent service. These areas are often geographically isolated from the subway network, requiring long wait times or multiple transfers to access other boroughs.

No-access zones

There are fewer notable access voids in New York City, with the very few found only on the outskirts of the city.

City outskirts

Why the voids still matter. Although not always high-poverty zones, these areas may still represent vulnerable populations, such as the elderly, disabled, or transit-dependent, who face disproportionate burdens due to geographic exclusion.

This spatial mismatch between need and service provision echoes prior findings from Jiao and Dillivan (2013) on transit deserts, and it calls into question how effectively federally funded transit investments are meeting their equity objectives.

The question

Do lower-income communities in these cities receive the same level of access to frequent and reliable federally funded transit services as wealthier communities?

Public transportation is often championed as a cornerstone of equitable urban life, providing affordable access to employment, education, health care, and social networks. In theory, robust transit infrastructure funded by federal investment should serve all residents equitably. However, in practice, access to public transit, especially reliable and frequent service, remains profoundly uneven.

This project builds on transportation equity literature that critiques stop proximity as a sole measure of accessibility. Manaugh and El-Geneidy (2012) emphasize that transit frequency is a crucial determinant of effective mobility, one that is often unequally distributed across income and racial lines. Karner and Niemeier (2013) argue that service provision must account for demographic disparities, not just geographic coverage.

How it was measured

Three layers, built in ArcGIS Pro

Each layer on the map comes from a separate analysis of GTFS schedules and ACS census data.

Service frequency

Parsed weekday schedules, trips, and stop times to calculate average wait times at each stop. Stops averaging over 20 minutes were classified as low-frequency, then spatially joined to census tracts to count low-frequency stops per tract.

Headway > 20 min

No-transit-access zones

Buffered every bus and rail stop by 0.5 miles and every ferry stop by 0.75 miles, reflecting common planning standards for walkable access. Areas outside all buffers were dissolved into polygons and mapped as red diamonds.

0.5 mi · 0.75 mi ferry

Household income

Layered the most recent American Community Survey median household income onto the maps in five classes along a yellow-to-blue sequential ramp, linking income to transit conditions at a glance.

Evaluation

Limitations & next steps

Limitations

  • Weekday service onlyWeekend, evening, and early morning schedules are excluded, though those are times when low-income and shift workers may be most dependent on transit.
  • Scheduled, not real-timeGTFS provides scheduled headways but not real-time performance or on-time reliability, which are crucial to user experience.
  • Fixed buffer distancesNo-access zones may not reflect pedestrian realities such as sidewalk quality, elevation, or safety.
  • No funding data yetActual funding allocation data is needed to determine whether service inequities correspond to disparities in federal investment.

Next steps

  • Follow the moneyIntegrate Federal Transit Administration (FTA) and National Transit Database (NTD) data to track funding across agencies, routes, and neighborhoods.
  • Widen the equity lensAdd demographic layers beyond income, such as race and car ownership.
  • Expand and compare over timeInclude other U.S. metros, or use historic GTFS feeds to see whether access is improving or worsening.
  • Validate with behaviorUse ridership or mobile device travel flows to test whether poorly served areas also show underutilization or suppressed demand.

Conclusion

Public transit access, even in cities with expansive systems like New York and San Francisco, is far from equitable. The presence of a bus stop does not guarantee timely, reliable access to the urban fabric. True transit equity must account for service frequency, walkability, user demographics, and infrastructure funding.

Tools like the Transit Inequity Dashboard not only make these disparities visible but also challenge planners and policymakers to close the service gaps that persist in America's most transit-dependent cities.

The road to equitable mobility requires targeted, data-driven investment in the communities that have been left waiting.
